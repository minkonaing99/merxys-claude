## Introduction

Tn the winter of 2011 I began keeping a list of the ways my own thinking went wrong. It started as a private document and became this book.

The errors collected here are systematic, not random. That is what makes them worth studying: a bias that fires the same way every time can be anticipated, while a random slip cannot.

Avoiding stupidity is easier than seeking brilliance, and it compounds just as reliably.

## The Anchor Trap

Anchoring

People latch on to the first number they hear and adjust from it, never far enough. A jury told a large opening demand awards more than one told a small demand, though the case is identical. Negotiators exploit this by speaking first.

In conclusion: name the effect and you have already blunted it.

## Why Crowds Go Wrong

## Social Proof

We treat the behavior of others as evidence about reality, which works until everyone is copying everyone. Tn a panic the crowd becomes an amplifier rather than a sensor. Ask what the crowd knows that you do not before joining it.

In conclusion: a crowd is evidence only when its members looked independently.

## The Cost You Cannot Recover

Sunk Cost Fallacy

Money already spent should not shape the next decision, yet it reliably does. We continue failing projects to avoid admitting the earlier spend was wasted. The only question that matters is what the next dollar buys.

In conclusion: name the effect and you have already blunted it.

## Accomplished

Premature Closure

This chapter's title was split across two headings by the scanner and must be merged back into one. Declaring victory early changes how an organization behaves for years afterward. The label sticks long after the facts contradict it.

In conclusion: the flag planted early is the hardest one to take down.

## Seeing Patterns That Are Not There

Clustering Illusion

Random data contains streaks, and our pattern detector fires on all of them. Coin flips produce long runs that feel meaningful and are not. Before naming a pattern, ask what randomness alone would have produced.

In conclusion: name the effect and you have already blunted it.

## The Expert Who Knew Too Little

Overconfidence Effect

Confidence and accuracy come apart badly, and experts are not spared. Forecasters state narrow ranges that the outcome misses far more often than their stated certainty allows. Widen every range you are tempted to give.

In conclusion: name the effect and you have already blunted it.

## Epilogue

The list is not complete and never will be. New errors surface as fast as old ones are catalogued.

What changes is not the number of biases you can name but how quickly you notice one operating on you.
