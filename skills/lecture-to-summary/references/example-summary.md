Use it to calibrate depth and tone, not to copy structure literally — the section order is fixed by SKILL.md. This example includes the optional **Assignment** section because the lecture set one; skip that section when a lecture doesn't.

Note how it fuses sources: the grading breakdown came from slides, the AI-usage rule and the exact deadline came from the transcript, and the thesis-topic questions were in the user's notes. The prose is written in the user's own voice via the `my-writing-tone` skill, not generic AI phrasing. Roughly one screen. Logistics chatter (screen-share fumbles) dropped.

# Lecture 1 — Introduction to Research Methodology

> [[index]]

## TL;DR
First class of the research methodology course. Sets up how the course works (learn-by-doing, graded on assignments not exams), the arc of a research project from topic to publication, and how to start finding a thesis topic. Key practical takeaway: AI may be used only for grammar checking, never to write your report.

## Deadlines & action items
- [ ] Assignment 1 — due 2026-08-21 23:59 (before next class)
- [ ] Next class — onsite, Sat 2026-08-22
- [ ] Begin searching for a thesis / independent-study topic

## Assignment
Assignment 1: write up two topics of interest in Cybersecurity / Information Assurance — one in my (expected) advisor's area, one about AI.

Each topic needs:
- A topic sentence in Turabian form — Topic ("I am working on X"), Question ("because I want to find out Y"), Significance ("so that I can help others understand/improve Z").
- A plain-language explanation of the topic and its technical terms, so a non-specialist gets it.

When I present it: explain the terms first, then the topic (most people do it backwards and lose the room). Submitting on time = full marks; feedback only, no deductions, so it's safe to pick something ambitious. This assignment seeds my later project, so a good topic choice here saves rework.

## Key concepts
- **Research process** — the standard arc of a project: find topic → literature review → methodology → implement/experiment → analyse results → dissemination (publish). Maps onto the course's later lectures.
- **Hypothesis** — a guessed answer based on reasons and prior knowledge. Example given: "Is authentication method X more convenient and secure than face auth for a banking app?" → H: yes.
- **Conceptual framework & research design** — planning what data to gather and how (experiments, surveys, reusing others' data) before collecting anything.
- **Computer-science research specifically** — building new artifacts (algorithm, system, theory), defining their properties, measuring how they perform, and applying them to the real world.

## What you need to know
- Grading is assignment-driven: assignments + presentations + class participation, 30% final report, 10% final presentation. Each ~20% component is expected to score above 80%.
- AI-usage rule (Faculty level 2 = planning only): you may NOT copy AI-generated content into your report. Grammar checking (Grammarly / Grammarly-style, QuillBot) is allowed. AI writing gets flagged and rejected — you redo it. Reason given: "writing is thinking."
- You are expected to actively search for your own thesis topic starting now; the professor won't hand you one.

## Open questions / gaps
- Exact percentage split of assignments vs presentations vs participation (only 30% report + 10% presentation stated explicitly).
- Which specific AI tools count as acceptable "grammar check" — to be detailed in the later report-writing lecture.
